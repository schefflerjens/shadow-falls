## Tracking Dust and Tequila

The alley behind the hardware store was still hot. The iron fire escape ticked in the heat. I ducked under the yellow tape I'd strung across the basement entrance and went down first, one hand on the wall, the stairs complaining the same way they had an hour ago.

Charlaine followed with the canvas bag.

At the bottom, the light was the same thin wedge from the ground-level vent. The smell was the same — stagnant, dusty, old. The paint cans. The collapsed shelving. The concrete floor where I'd put both men down.

I stood in the center of the room and let my eyes adjust.

"Here," I said. "And here." I pointed to the two spots. The concrete was scuffed where they'd gone down. A dark smear near the far wall where the second one had slid when I hit him.

Charlaine set the canvas bag on a section of clear floor and unzipped it. The kit made a sound like glass wind chimes when she opened it. She pulled out a small cloth pouch, drawstring tied at the top, and a wide-mouthed jar with a cork stopper. The jar held powder. Pale lavender, fine as talc, with a faint shimmer to it that caught even this weak light.

"Grand-Mère's tracking dust," she said. "It finds residue from structured workings. Anything deliberate, anything with real architecture behind it."

"I know what it does."

"I'm narrating for myself." She pulled the cork and tipped a careful line of powder across her palm. "Don't rush me."

I didn't rush her.

She moved through the basement in a slow circuit, palm held flat, the powder sitting still on her skin. She passed the paint cans. The shelving. The dark smear on the wall. She came back around to the center of the room and stopped.

The powder moved.

Not much. A lean, a drift, the way iron filings move toward a magnet. It pulled toward the far corner, the one behind the collapsed shelving. Charlaine followed it. I followed her.

The shelving had come down in a heap — old metal brackets, warped particleboard, the whole thing folded in on itself like a bad hand of cards. Charlaine crouched and held her palm over the debris. The powder pulled harder now, the shimmer brightening to something almost visible in the dark.

"There," she said.

I shifted two sections of shelving by hand. Under the second piece, half-buried in the grit and the dark, was a small object. Leather. Circular. About the size of a silver dollar.

I picked it up and held it toward the vent light.

A rosette. Stitched leather, worn soft with age, the edges going pale where the dye had rubbed away. The front had a stylized claw motif — three curved lines pressed into the leather, clean work, well-made. Around the edge, in small stamped letters: *FANGS & FUR — 50 YEARS.*

I turned it over. Nothing on the back.

"Somebody dropped it," Charlaine said. "Or it was on one of them when they were pulled."

"Either way, it was here when the working happened." I closed my fist around it. "The residue attached to it."

Charlaine's powder had gone still again. She tipped it back into the jar and corked it. "That's your trace," she said. "That's what was left."

I opened my wallet. Behind the badge card and the folded city ordinance I kept for arguments, there was a leather rosette. Same claw motif. Same stamped lettering. Same worn edges.

I held them side by side.

"You have one," Charlaine said.

"Millie gave them out at the anniversary party. Everybody got one." I looked at the two rosettes. "Everybody who was there."

---

Fangs and Fur sat at the end of Crescent Street, where the pavement went to gravel and the streetlights gave up. The building was old timber and river stone, two stories, with a covered porch that ran the full width of the front. Light leaked out from every seam. The sound hit you from half a block away — steel guitar, a bass line, the low roar of a room full of people who had decided the evening was worth something.

I pushed through the double doors.

The warmth came first. Then the smell — whiskey and woodsmoke and the particular density of a crowd. Oil lamps hung from the ceiling beams, throwing that honeyed light across the worn floorboards. Every booth was full. The bar ran the length of the left wall, stools packed tight. A stage at the back held a three-piece band working through something in a minor key. The trophies watched from the walls — elk, boar, something with too many horns to be strictly natural.

A table near the stage had a pair of shifters in it, their eyes tracking the room the way eyes do when they're built for it. A booth by the window held a figure that took up the whole bench — Grog, an ogre the size of a hay bale, nursing a ceramic stein with both hands and watching the band with the focused attention of a man who takes his music seriously. Across the room, a woman in a clerical collar was arguing theology with someone whose teeth were too long for the conversation.

Charlaine leaned close. "The usual crowd."

"The usual crowd," I said.

We took two stools at the bar. Millie appeared in front of us before we'd settled. She had the weathered face and the easy smile she always had, the kind of warmth that comes from decades of meaning it. A bar rag over one shoulder. A clean glass in one hand.

"Jam." She set the glass down. "You look like you went twelve rounds with a gravel road."

"Ten rounds," I said. "The road quit first."

She poured tequila without being asked. Good tequila, the kind she kept on the second shelf. She set it in front of me and looked at Charlaine.

"Cappuccino," Charlaine said.

Millie's smile held, but something behind it went flat. "This ain't no Starbucks, cher."

"I know." Charlaine smiled back. "But you've got that machine."

A pause. Millie looked at the espresso machine at the end of the bar — chrome, Italian, comically out of place between the whiskey bottles and the jar of pickled eggs. She'd acquired it three years ago and had never fully forgiven herself for it.

"Fine," Millie said, and went to make the cappuccino with the dignity of a woman who had made her choices and would live with them.

I drank the tequila. It hit the clay the way it always did — not warmth, exactly, but something close to it. A loosening. I set the glass down and looked at the room.

Charlaine had the rosette out. She worked the bar, moving from stool to stool, holding it up, asking questions. I watched her work. She had a way of asking things that didn't feel like asking — easy, conversational, the kind of manner that made people answer before they'd decided whether to. Her grandmother had taught her that. Or maybe she'd come by it naturally. Hard to say.

Nobody recognized the rosette.

Millie set the cappuccino down and Charlaine came back to her stool. She wrapped both hands around the cup and shook her head once.

"Nothing," she said. "Nobody placed it."

"Somebody placed it in that basement."

"I know." She drank. "We need Krabat."

I already knew that. I didn't like it, but I already knew it.

"He'll be here," I said. "He's always here."

---

He came in twenty minutes later.

I didn't see him come through the door. He was simply not there, and then he was — at the far end of the bar, dark jacket, white hair doing what it always did, those black eyes already moving across the room the way a man's eyes move when he wants to know the exits before he knows the people. He found me in about four seconds. He always did.

He took the stool two down from mine and signaled Millie with two fingers. She poured without asking.

"Sheriff," he said. His accent was faint, old-world, the kind that had been in America long enough to go soft at the edges but never fully disappear.

"Krabat."

He looked at Charlaine. "Deputy." A small nod. Courteous, the way a man is courteous when it costs him nothing.

Charlaine slid the rosette down the bar toward him.

He looked at it. He didn't touch it immediately. He looked at it the way you look at something you want to assess before you commit to a reaction.

Then he picked it up.

He turned it over. Examined the claw motif. Ran his thumb across the stamped lettering. His face gave nothing.

"Fangs and Fur," he said. "Fiftieth anniversary. Millie threw a good party."

"You were there," I said.

"I was there." He set the rosette back on the bar. "So were you. So was half the town."

"We found it in the basement of the old hardware building on Maple Lane. Under a pile of shelving. With teleportation residue on it."

He picked up his drink. "Interesting."

"That's all you've got?"

"It's a genuine response." He sipped. "Someone lost a keepsake. Someone who attends local functions. That narrows your field to roughly two hundred people."

"Someone who can pull two unconscious men off a public street in broad daylight."

"That narrows it considerably." He set the glass down. "Though not to me, if that's the direction you're heading."

"I'm not heading anywhere yet. I'm collecting information."

"Then I'll give you some." He turned on the stool to face me. "The working you're describing — the signature, the architecture — that's old. Older than this town. Older than most things in this country." His eyes were flat and steady. "Whoever did it didn't learn from a book. They learned from someone who learned from someone. There's a lineage there."

"Can you trace it?"

"Possibly." He picked up the rosette again. Held it between two fingers. "Given time."

"I have two days."

Something moved in his expression. Not surprise. Something more like recognition. "The Bureau."

"Thursday."

He set the rosette down and finished his drink. "Then you should move quickly."

That was it. That was all he was giving me. I looked at him for a long moment and he looked back, pleasant and opaque and giving nothing.

---

The fight started at the table by the stage.

Bruno was a regular — heavyset, human, the kind of man who got louder as the night went on and thought that was the same as getting funnier. He'd said something to Grog. I didn't hear what it was. I heard Grog's response, which shook the table and turned heads across the room.

Bruno stood up. Grog stood up. The difference in scale was significant.

I was off the stool before the first shove.

"Bruno." I put myself between them. Bruno was red in the face and breathing through his nose. Grog had the look of a man who has decided to be patient for exactly three more seconds. "Grog."

"He said—" Bruno started.

"I don't need to know what he said." I put a hand on Bruno's chest. "Sit down."

"He started it."

"Millie." I didn't turn around. "Two glasses of the Patrón Silver. The good one. On me."

A pause. Then Millie's voice, dry as dust: "Coming up."

Bruno looked at the glasses when they arrived. He looked at Grog. Grog looked at the glasses. The math was simple. Good tequila was better than a fight, and both of them knew it.

Bruno sat down.

Grog sat down.

The band started up again.

I let out a breath and turned back to the bar.

The stool where Charlaine had been sitting was empty.

The stool where Krabat had been sitting was empty.

I stood there for a moment. The cappuccino cup was still on the bar, half-full, the foam gone flat. Krabat's glass was empty and dry.

The smell hit me then. Faint, already fading, the way it had on Maple Lane.

Clove.

Ozone.

I pressed my palm flat against the surface of the bar where Charlaine had been sitting. The wood was cold. Cold the way concrete gets cold when something reaches in and takes what it wants.

Millie was watching me from the other end of the bar.

"Did you see—" I started.

"One second she was there," Millie said. Her voice had gone careful. "Then she wasn't."

I stood at the bar with my hand flat on the cold wood. The band played. The crowd talked. The oil lamps threw their honey-colored light across the room and nobody in it knew what had just happened.

The rosette was still on the bar. Krabat had left it behind.

I picked it up and put it in my pocket with the other one.

Two days before the Bureau arrived. Two people gone in the same working that had taken the twins. And the only lead I had was a worn leather keepsake and a cold spot on a bar top in a room full of people who hadn't seen a thing.

I signaled Millie.

"Another tequila," I said.

She poured it. I drank it standing up. It didn't help the way I needed it to.

It never did.
