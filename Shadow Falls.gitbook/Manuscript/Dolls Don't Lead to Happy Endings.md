## The Cabin

The trees closed in fast.

Twenty yards past the tree line, the streetlights were gone. The canopy was dense enough that the sky disappeared in patches, leaving only black spaces between branches and the occasional gray smear of cloud. My shoes found soft ground — pine needles, the occasional root that wanted to put me down. I watched the doll in the petri dish and kept moving.

It pointed steady. No hesitation. No drift.

The air changed as I went deeper. Cooler, yes — but something else underneath the cool. The way air gets in rooms that have been shut up too long. Stale at the edges. Pressurized, almost. Like the forest was holding something in.

My joints were still stiff at the hip from the afternoon. The clay at my knuckles had that two-sizes-too-small feeling. I'd had three mugs of coffee and two shots of tequila and neither had done what I needed. I was running on the kind of tired that sits below the bones.

I kept walking.

The doll turned in its dish.

I stopped.

It had been pointing west since unit eleven. Now it had shifted — maybe ten degrees north. I adjusted my bearing and kept going, stepping over a fallen birch, ducking under a low branch.

The cabin appeared the way bad things appear in the dark — not all at once, but in pieces. First the shape of it, a geometry that didn't belong in the forest. Then the outline, a low structure leaning slightly to the right, the way old buildings lean when the foundation has given up on one side. Then the details, as I got closer, as my eyes pulled what light there was and worked with it.

Paint peeling from the clapboards in long strips, the wood beneath gone silver-gray. Windows boarded over with planks nailed at irregular angles. A porch with two steps, both rotted through. A door swollen in its frame and forced shut. A rusted tin roof with a hole in one corner, the edges curled back like a can opener had gotten at it.

The kind of place that had been abandoned long enough to forget it had ever been inhabited.

The doll pointed at the front door.

I crouched behind a pine trunk and looked at the perimeter.

They were there. I counted six before I stopped counting. Same face on each of them — medium height, medium build, brown hair, the forgettable features I'd been seeing since the rooftop on Maple Lane. They moved the perimeter in a pattern, slow and mechanical, the way a clock hand moves. No communication between them. No variation. They didn't need to coordinate. They were running the same program.

I'd seen that face twice before. Once on the roof, once in the basement. Two men I'd assumed were twins, which was unlikely enough. Now there were six of them, and unlikely didn't begin to cover it. These weren't brothers. They weren't even people. They were constructs — built things, shaped from material and intent and the same structured magic I'd been smelling for two days. Krabat's work. The same hand that had pulled Charlaine out of her chair at the station and dropped her somewhere in these woods.

Legba had told me who had her. Now I was going to get her back.

Six of them. One of me, stiff at the hip, short a night's sleep, running on tequila and the particular stubbornness that comes from being made of earth.

I put the petri dish in my breast pocket next to the Jack of Crossroads.

The first one came around the east side of the cabin on a regular circuit. I waited until he was eight feet out, stepped from behind the pine, and hit him once across the jaw with my right hand. The impact was solid. He went sideways, hit the ground, and lay still. No sound. No reaction from the others. They were running their circuits, not watching each other.

I dragged him behind the pine and left him there.

The second one came from the north side. I let him pass the corner of the cabin and took him from behind — arm around the throat, a sharp torque, the same move I'd used in the basement. He went limp. I set him against the cabin wall where the shadows were deepest.

The third one saw me.

He didn't hesitate. No fear, no calculation — he came straight in, fast, and he was strong. Stronger than the two I'd taken quietly. He got both hands on my lapels and drove me backward into the cabin wall, and the rotted clapboards gave way with a crack like a rifle shot. We went through the wall together.

Inside the cabin, the dark was total.

He had my lapels. I had his wrists. We stood in the wreckage of the wall, splinters around our feet, and he was trying to drive me further back while I held my ground. He was strong the way a machine is strong — no variance, no fatigue, constant pressure. I felt the clay at my shoulders compress under the grip.

I let him push. Let him build momentum. Then I dropped my weight and turned, and he went over my hip and into the far wall. The wall held. He didn't.

He slid down and stayed down.

The fourth and fifth came through the door at the same time.

The cabin was one room. Low ceiling, bare floor, a dead fireplace on the far wall. Enough gray light came through the gaps in the boarded windows to make out shapes. The two constructs spread apart as they came in, flanking, and I understood they'd been built for this — built to work in pairs when the situation called for it.

I went for the closer one first. Three steps and a straight right hand to the center of his face. He dropped. The second one caught me from the side, a solid hit to the ribs that would have cracked bone if I had any. Instead it compressed the clay, deformed it, the impact spreading through my torso like a shockwave through wet sand. I felt myself go soft at the midsection — not painful, exactly, but wrong. A structural wrongness.

I grabbed his arm before he could pull it back. Twisted. Kept twisting until he went down.

Five.

The sixth one was waiting by the fireplace. He wasn't on a circuit. He'd been standing there, still as furniture, and I'd walked past him in the dark.

He hit me from behind. Both fists, the back of my head, a blow that would have put a man down for good. My head snapped forward. The clay at the base of my skull compressed and cracked — I heard it, felt it, the grinding sensation of fractured clay that hadn't been moistened in too long. My vision went white for half a second.

I turned around.

He hit me again. Same place. Same force. Working methodically, the way you work when you know what you're doing.

I grabbed his collar and walked him backward into the fireplace. The stone was solid. He wasn't. He hit the mantle and folded at the knees and went down into the ash.

I stood in the middle of the cabin.

Six down. The room smelled like old wood and something else — the sharp, layered smell I'd been tracking for two days. Clove. Ozone. The smell of structured magic, worked and reworked in this space, built into the walls and the floor and the air itself.

The place felt staged. All of it — the cabin, the perimeter, the six constructs running their circuits. A trap I'd walked into because the doll had pointed here and I hadn't had a better option. Krabat had known I'd come. He'd left a trail and I'd followed it because the alternative was doing nothing, and doing nothing meant Charlaine stayed wherever she was.

I'd walked into it anyway. That was the job.

The floor was bare boards, gray with age, the gaps between them wide enough to see through. I moved to the center of the room and crouched, running my palm along the planks. Most of them were solid. Nailed down, swollen into place, the kind of floor that had been there since the cabin was built.

Near the fireplace, one board gave.

I pressed it again. A fraction of movement, a give that the others didn't have. I got my fingers into the gap and pulled.

The board came up. Then the next one. Then the next. Under them, a trapdoor — iron hinges, a recessed handle, the wood of it newer than the floor above. Someone had cut this after the cabin was built. Recently enough that the cut edges of the boards hadn't weathered.

I pulled the handle.

The trapdoor swung up on well-oiled hinges. No resistance. No creak. Someone had maintained this.

Below was a ladder, wooden rungs, dropping into a lit space. The light was low and orange, the color of a kerosene lamp. The air that came up was damp and cold and smelled of earth.

I went down.

---

The chamber was maybe fifteen feet square. Dirt walls, timber-braced, the ceiling low enough that I had to duck at the center beam. A kerosene lamp hung from an iron hook in the corner, throwing orange light across the packed earth floor. A workbench against one wall, covered in objects I didn't look at yet. Shelving on the opposite wall, jars and pouches and things wrapped in cloth. The smell was heavy — old earth, clove, ozone, and something underneath it all that I recognized as the smell of active workings, the smell of magic used recently and often.

She was against the far wall.

Sitting on the floor, back against the timber bracing, wrists bound in front of her with something that looked like braided cord but shimmered when the lamplight caught it. Her head was down, the short curls dusty and tangled. Her jacket was gone. Her uniform shirt was torn at the shoulder. There was dried blood on her temple, a cut that had stopped bleeding but hadn't been cleaned.

I crossed the chamber in four steps and crouched in front of her.

"Charlaine."

Her head came up. The eyes took a moment to focus — the slow, dragging focus of someone coming up from a long way down. Then they found me and went sharp.

"Jam." Her voice was rough. Dry. "What—" She stopped. Blinked. Looked at the chamber, at the ladder, at me. "What happened?"

"Later." I looked at the cord on her wrists. The shimmer was a working — binding magic, the kind that would tighten if she pulled against it. I didn't pull against it. I pressed my thumb to the center of the braid and held it there, feeling for the architecture the way you feel for a knot in the dark.

There was a knot. Layered, deliberate, the same signature I'd been tracking since the basement on Maple Lane. I found the outermost thread of it and pressed harder.

The cord went slack.

I unwound it from her wrists. The skin underneath was red, abraded, the marks of something that had been there for hours.

"Can you stand?"

"Yes." She said it before she tried. When she tried, her legs shook. I put a hand under her arm and she got up.

"How long?" I said.

"I don't know." She looked at the lamp. "It's been dark since they brought me here. No windows." She flexed her hands, wincing at the circulation coming back. "Krabat?"

"Krabat."

She absorbed that. Her jaw set. "I knew something was wrong when he sat down. He had the rosette in his hand and he was looking at it like—" She stopped. "Then the smell. Then nothing."

"He pulled you the same way he pulled the twins off Maple Lane."

"Structured working." Her eyes went to the workbench. "Jam. Look at this."

I looked.

The workbench held a collection of objects arranged with the same deliberate care I'd seen in the staged murder scenes. A shallow iron bowl, blackened inside, with a residue dried to a dark crust at the bottom. Three candles burned down to stubs, the wax pooled and hardened. A length of cord identical to the one I'd taken off her wrists, coiled in a tight spiral. At the center of it all, a disk of carved bone, maybe six inches across, covered in the same geometry I'd seen cut into the floor of unit eleven.

The inscription. The same pattern. But this one was three-dimensional — carved into the bone in relief, the lines sharp and deep, the whole thing mounted on a wooden stand so it faced the room.

The anchor. The instrument Legba had described. The thing they'd been using to tear the membrane.

I took it off the stand. The bone was cold. Not the ambient cold of the chamber — colder. The cold of a thing that had been used as a doorway too many times.

"Don't touch the carved side," Charlaine said.

"I know."

"Just checking." She looked at the ladder. "We need to move."

She was right. The six constructs I'd put down were still down, but I didn't know how long that held. I didn't know if Krabat had more of them. I didn't know if he'd been watching the cabin and already knew I was here.

I wrapped the bone disk in cloth from the workbench shelf and put it in my jacket pocket. It sat heavy against my ribs.

"Up," I said.

---

The cabin was still when we came up through the trapdoor. The six constructs were where I'd left them — three inside, two by the door, one in the fireplace ash. None had moved.

Charlaine looked at them. Looked at me.

"Six," she said.

"Six."

"You look terrible."

"You look worse."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Fair."

We went out through the hole in the east wall — the one the third construct and I had made together — and into the night air. The forest was dark and quiet around the cabin. No movement in the trees. No sound beyond the ordinary forest sounds, the insects, the wind in the high branches.

I pulled the petri dish from my breast pocket. The doll was still inside, still pointing — but now it had rotated a full one-eighty. Pointing back the way I'd come. Back toward Shadow Falls.

"Legba's compass," Charlaine said. She'd seen the dish before, in the storage unit, when I'd set it on the floor and watched it turn.

"It found you." I put it back in my pocket. "Now it wants to go home."

We moved through the trees at a pace Charlaine could manage. She was steadier than she'd been in the chamber, the circulation back in her legs, the fog of the binding wearing off. She kept pace without complaint. She watched the trees.

We were forty yards from the cabin when the sound started.

Low. Rhythmic. Footsteps on packed earth, multiple sets, moving in a pattern I recognized. The mechanical, clockwork rhythm of constructs running their circuits.

I stopped.

Charlaine stopped beside me.

Through the trees, back at the cabin, shapes moved in the dark. Same medium height, same medium build, the same forgettable faces catching the faint light. Not six. More than six. I counted eight before I stopped.

"He reset them," Charlaine said, low.

"Or he sent more."

They were moving outward from the cabin in a slow spiral, the way a search pattern expands. Patient. They had time. We didn't.

One of them turned in our direction.

I put a hand on Charlaine's arm and we went still, pressed against a pine trunk, not breathing. The construct's head moved, tracking. The forgettable face was blank and searching. Fifteen feet between us and it.

It took a step toward us.

The petri dish went warm in my breast pocket.

Not the warmth of the Jack of Crossroads — different. Brighter. A heat that pressed through the glass and the cloth of my jacket and sat against my ribs like a coal.

I pulled it out.

The doll was standing.

Not lying on its side. Standing, upright, balanced on the floor of the dish, the carved face turned toward me. As I watched, it grew. Not fast — a slow, steady expansion, the wood going soft and then not-wood, the material changing as the size changed, the carved lines becoming contours, the painted face becoming a face.

I took the lid off.

The figure stepped out of the dish onto my palm. Stepped off my palm onto the ground. Stepped away from me into the dark between the trees.

Charlaine made a sound beside me. Small. Involuntary.

The figure walking away from us was Charlaine. Her height, her build, her short curls, her torn uniform shirt. Walking at a normal pace, no hurry, directly toward the nearest construct. The construct turned. Tracked. Followed.

The other constructs turned.

The figure that was Charlaine and wasn't Charlaine moved through the trees at a steady pace, and eight constructs moved with it, following, their circuits broken, their attention locked on the thing that looked like the thing they'd been sent to keep.

They moved north. Away from us. Away from the road.

I looked at the empty petri dish in my hand. The doll was gone. Just a glass dish with a lid.

Charlaine was staring at the space where her double had walked into the dark.

"Grand-Mère," she said, very quietly.

I didn't ask what she meant. I pocketed the dish and put my hand on her shoulder.

"Car's east," I said. "Move."

---

We came out of the trees onto packed dirt, then gravel, then the road. My car was where I'd left it, parked on the shoulder two hundred yards east of the tree line, half on the grass. An old Ford, dark blue, the paint oxidized to something closer to gray. I'd had it for twenty years and it ran because it knew better than to stop.

I got Charlaine into the passenger seat and went around to the driver's side. The key was in my jacket pocket. I got it in the ignition on the second try — my right hand was stiff again, the knuckles dragging.

The engine turned over.

I pulled onto the road and pointed east, toward the orange glow of Shadow Falls against the low clouds.

Charlaine had her head back against the seat. Her eyes were open, watching the tree line out the window. The cut on her temple had started to bleed again, slightly. She pressed the back of her hand against it without looking.

"Krabat wasn't there," she said.

"No."

"He set the cabin up and left."

"He's not done." I kept my eyes on the road. "The anchor's in my pocket. He'll want it back."

"Or he'll come for us directly."

"Maybe." The road curved east, the trees falling away on both sides, the first streetlights of the western edge of town appearing ahead. "He's been three steps ahead since the beginning. He let me find the cabin. He let me find you."

Charlaine turned her head to look at me. "You think he wanted you to take the anchor."

"I think he wanted me moving in a specific direction." The streetlights thickened. Pavement under the tires again. "I just don't know what's at the end of it yet."

She was quiet for a moment. "How did you know where to look?"

"Legba told me Krabat had you. The doll did the rest."

She turned toward me. The light from a passing streetlamp caught the dried blood at her temple, the dust in her curls. "You met Legba."

"Two days ago. He's the one who sent the doll."

She sat with that. Outside, the tree line thinned and the first gas station of the western edge slid past. "He never told me he was watching," she said.

"He watches everything."

She looked back out the window. "The doll," she said. "It's gone."

"Yes."

"Grand-Mère puts herself into things sometimes. Into objects, into workings. To watch. To help when she's needed." She looked at her hands, the red marks still visible on her wrists. "She's been watching this whole time."

I thought about the doll standing upright in the dish. The slow expansion. The figure walking into the dark with eight constructs following.

"She's good," I said.

"Don't tell her that," Charlaine said. "She already knows."

The station appeared on the left, City Hall dark above it, one window lit on the second floor. Anna's window. Still burning at this hour, because Anna didn't go home when the town was in trouble. She didn't go home much at all.

I pulled to the curb and cut the engine.

The town was quiet around us. The streetlights. The distant sound of the river. The ordinary sounds of a place that had gone to bed without knowing what had happened in the woods at its edge.

Charlaine opened the door and stepped out. She stood on the sidewalk and looked up at the lit window.

"Anna knows?" she said.

"Anna knows."

She nodded. Pressed her hand to the cut on her temple again. "I need coffee."

"There's a pot on the counter."

"Is it burnt?"

"It's always burnt."

She looked at me over the roof of the car. The streetlight caught the dust in her curls, the dried blood at her temple, the torn shoulder of her shirt. She looked like someone who had been taken and come back, which was exactly what she was.

"Jam." Her voice was even. "Whatever deal you made to find me—"

"Later," I said.

She held my gaze for a moment. Then she nodded once and went inside.

I sat in the car with the engine off. The anchor was in my pocket, cold through the cloth. The Jack of Crossroads was in my breast pocket, still warm. Two rosettes in my jacket pocket, worn leather, the claw motif pressed into the surface.

Two days. One night gone.

Krabat was out there somewhere. The membrane was still torn. The Bureau was coming Thursday whether I was ready or not.

I got out of the car and went inside.

The coffee was burnt.

I drank it anyway.
