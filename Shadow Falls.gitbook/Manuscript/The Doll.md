The walk back to City Hall was quiet. The heat had finally broken, the sun gone behind the tree line, leaving the air thick and still. My shoes were dusty. My jacket was dusty. I probably looked like something that had been buried and dug back up, which wasn't entirely inaccurate.

I had a playing card in my breast pocket, two rosettes in my jacket pocket, and a wooden doll the size of my thumb in my left hand. I kept the doll closed in my fist. It kept trying to turn.

The staircase to Anna's office creaked on the fourth step and the seventh. I stepped over both.

The frosted glass was lit. It was always lit, this late in a bad week.

I knocked once and went in.

---

The office had gotten worse since the afternoon. The city budget printout had been joined by a second one, spread over the first at an angle, both of them covered in red pen. The towers of case files on the credenza had multiplied. A cold cup of coffee sat on the corner of the desk next to a bottle of aspirin with the cap off. The brass lamp still threw its tight circle of yellow light, and everything outside that circle was dark.

Anna was at her desk. She'd taken her hair down. It sat in a loose knot now, held with a pencil instead of the letter opener. The reading glasses were on. She had a pen in one hand and a file in the other, and she looked up when I came in with the eyes of a woman who had been awake since five in the morning and intended to be awake until midnight and didn't see any way around it.

She looked at my face. Then at my hand.

"Sit," she said.

I sat.

She set the file down. She took off her glasses. She folded them and put them on the stack of journals and gave me her full attention, the way she did when she knew she wasn't going to like what came next.

"Talk," she said.

I talked.

I laid it out in order. The inscription under Frankie's blood — old cuts in the concrete, geometry that didn't come from casual work. The way my hand moved before I'd decided to move it. The cold. The void opening up from the floor of unit eleven like a door someone had been waiting to unlock.

Anna didn't move.

I told her about the hound. The red eyes. The size of it, the silence of it, the way it sat when it had decided I wasn't worth eating yet. I told her about the figure with the straw hat and the tattered coat and the voice like erosion. The walking stick. The shadow where his face should have been.

She picked up the cold coffee. Put it down without drinking.

I told her about the deal. Find the anchor — whatever instrument they'd been using to tear the membrane between this place and the void. Bring it to Legba. In exchange, he'd tell me where she was.

Anna's eyes sharpened. "She?"

I reached into my jacket and took out the two rosettes and set them on the desk. Then I reached into my breast pocket and took out the Jack of Crossroads and set it beside them. It was still warm. Anna looked at it without touching it.

"He had a photograph," I said. "Old stock. Sepia. A man and a woman in front of a building I didn't recognize." I kept my voice flat. "I knew the woman's face."

The room was quiet. The lamp hummed. Outside, somewhere on Main Street, a car passed.

"Forty years," Anna said. Not a question.

"Forty years."

She looked at me for a moment. Not with pity. Anna didn't do pity. Something closer to the look you give a wound when you're deciding whether it needs stitches.

"He's holding it over you," she said.

"Yes."

"And you agreed to the deal anyway."

"I agreed to the deal." I put my hands flat on my knees. "I don't trust him. He's a crossroads god. He deals in leverage and passage and things that cost more than they look like they cost. But he's right that someone is tearing the membrane. He's right that I'm already in the middle of it. And the photograph—"

I stopped.

Forty years was a long time to look for someone. Long enough that the looking had become its own habit, its own weight. Long enough that I'd stopped expecting to find her and started expecting to carry the not-finding. And then a god in a straw hat had held a piece of paper in a cold void and the whole forty years had compressed into a single point.

"The photograph is something I can't ignore," I said.

Anna was quiet for a moment. She picked up the aspirin bottle and shook two into her palm and swallowed them dry. Then she looked at the Jack of Crossroads.

"And Charlaine?" she said.

I reached into my left jacket pocket and set the doll on the desk between us.

Anna looked at it. She picked it up carefully, the way you pick up something that might be fragile or might be dangerous and you haven't decided which yet. She turned it over. Read the name etched on the back. Her jaw tightened once, then relaxed.

"Legba gave you this?"

"Left it on the floor of the storage unit. It's a compass. It points to her."

Anna set the doll on the desk. We both watched it. It lay still for a moment. Then it rotated — slow, deliberate, the same forty-five degrees it had turned in unit eleven — and pointed toward the west wall of the office.

The window behind Anna's desk looked west. Beyond the glass, beyond the streetlights and the edge of town, the tree line was a dark mass against the last gray light of the sky.

Anna looked at the doll. She opened her desk drawer and took out a glass petri dish — the kind she used for samples, clean and clear, the lid sitting beside it. She set the doll inside the dish. It rotated again, settling on the same bearing.

She put the lid on.

The doll kept pointing west.

"Krabat," I said.

Anna's expression went still. Not surprise. She'd been running through the same list I had, and she'd gotten to the same name.

"Tell me why," she said.

"Legba said I'd already met him. Said he was sitting two stools down from me at Fangs and Fur." I looked at the petri dish. "Krabat was sitting two stools down. He handled the rosette. He left before I noticed the smell. Clove and ozone, same as Maple Lane, same as the storage unit." I paused. "He knew about the Bureau. He knew about the two-day window. He told me to move quickly."

"He told you to move quickly," Anna said.

"He knew exactly how much time I had. And he had a front-row seat to everything I knew and didn't know." I let that sit. "He's been three steps ahead the whole time. Not because he's been watching — because he's been steering."

Anna stood. She moved to the window and looked out at the dark tree line with her arms crossed and her back to the room. The lamp threw her shadow long across the wall.

The office was quiet. The aspirin bottle sat open on the desk. The budget printouts were covered in red ink. The cold coffee was getting colder.

She'd been running this town for twenty years. She'd kept the federal hands off the department, kept the charter loopholes intact, kept the money flowing to the places that needed it and away from the places that asked too many questions. She was tired in the way that people get tired when they've been holding something up for a long time and they're not going to put it down.

"Do you trust Legba?" she said. Her back was still to me.

"No."

"But you took the deal."

"I took the deal."

She turned around. Her face was composed, the exhaustion sitting underneath it rather than on top of it.

"The photograph," she said. "If this goes wrong — if Legba's deal costs more than it looks like it costs — you'll have spent forty years looking for her and lost Charlaine in the bargain."

I looked at the petri dish. The doll pointed west, steady as a compass needle.

"I know," I said.

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

Anna looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up the petri dish and held it out across the desk.

"Go bring them back," she said.

I took it.

I stood. My joints were stiff at the hip still, the clay not fully recovered from the afternoon. I moved carefully. At the door I stopped.

"If I'm not back before Thursday—"

"You'll be back before Thursday," Anna said. She'd already picked up her pen. The file was open again. "I didn't give you two days so you could miss the deadline."

I left her to it.

---

The stairs creaked on the fourth step and the seventh. I didn't step over them this time.

Outside, the night air was thick and warm, the last of the day's heat sitting in the pavement. The streetlights on Main Street threw their orange circles on the asphalt. The town was quiet. A few lights on in the houses. A dog barking somewhere on the east side. The ordinary sounds of a place that had gone to bed.

I held the petri dish in my left hand and walked west.

The doll pointed the same direction it had been pointing since unit eleven. Steady. Patient. The carved face had no expression, but there was intention in it, the kind of intention that gets carved into something when the person doing the carving knows what they want it to do.

The streetlights thinned as I hit the western edge of town. The pavement gave way to gravel, then to packed dirt, then to the soft ground of the tree line. The forest was dark and dense, the canopy closing out the sky. The air changed — cooler here, heavier, the smell of pine and wet earth replacing the dust of the street.

I stopped at the tree line.

The doll pointed straight ahead, into the dark.

I checked the Jack of Crossroads in my breast pocket. Still warm. I checked the revolver at my hip. Still there.

I thought about Charlaine's cappuccino, half-full and going flat on the bar.

I thought about the photograph. The woman's face looking straight at the camera. The building I didn't recognize. Forty years of not-finding compressed into a single held breath.

I thought about Krabat sitting two stools down, handling the rosette with careful hands, telling me to move quickly.

I went into the trees.
